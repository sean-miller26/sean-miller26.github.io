Deeper Engagement Exploration Tool

Role: Product Designer
Scope: Subject pages, tagging, exploration pathways, discussion touchpoints
Timeline: 8 weeks
Outcome: Clearer exploratory pathways, lowered barriers to participation

Designing for Serendipity Through Context and Exploration

Volunteers arrive at a subject because something caught their eye—a strange galaxy, an unusual signal, an unexpected pattern. But too often, that moment of intrigue hits a wall. The page shows the image, asks for a classification, and then… stops.

For a platform known for unexpected discoveries, our Subject pages weren't doing enough to support exploration, connection, or insight.

This case study explores how we reimagined the Subject page as a deeper engagement hub—one that invites volunteers to linger, investigate, and contribute thoughtfully.

The Tension

Zooniverse thrives on careful, genuine classifications. Quality matters more than speed.

Yet many volunteers—especially advanced ones—were already doing more than the interface supported. They opened external tools, cross-referenced catalogs, and built their own mental models of what they were seeing.

Meanwhile, casual users often sensed there was more to discover, but didn't know how to get there.

The challenge wasn't motivation. It was access.

The Challenge

How might we lower the barrier to deeper exploration while preserving the integrity of real scientific work?

Any solution needed to respect the realities of scientific work. Thoughtful engagement and careful observation matter far more than speed or volume, so our focus was on enabling curiosity—giving volunteers the tools, context, and pathways they needed to explore meaningfully, at their own pace.

What Was Broken

Sparse Interface

The Subject page was visually minimal, but at the cost of context. It missed opportunities to surface metadata and connections that could spark deeper investigation.

Disjointed Conversations

Comments were hard to find, poorly ordered, and separated from the subject itself—making discussion feel secondary rather than supportive.

No Clear Path Forward

There was no seamless way to move between observing a subject and classifying it. Exploration and contribution felt disconnected.

Goals

We set out to transform the Subject page into a space that supports multiple depths of engagement:

  • Make exploration and tagging easier and more inviting for casual users
  • Surface related subjects and patterns to encourage serendipitous discovery
  • Integrate key metadata and tools directly into the page for advanced users
  • Reduce friction between curiosity and contribution

Whether someone stayed for thirty seconds or thirty minutes, the page should reward their attention.

Guiding Principles

Engagement

Invite participation without pressure

Context Is Critical

Discovery requires understanding

Exploration

Encourage curiosity-driven pathways

Discovery

Support insight, not performance

The design favors clarity over reward, and meaning over metrics.

Research: Learning From Power Users

I led a cross-project brainstorming workshop with researchers to map overlapping needs and identify where Subject pages fell short.

I also interviewed advanced volunteers who regularly supplement classifications with external tools, including:

  • SIMBAD for object identification
  • VizieR for catalogs and photometry
  • WISEView for infrared imagery

These users weren't looking for incentives—they were looking for efficiency and context.

At the same time, research showed that while some volunteers hesitate to comment, tagging and metadata exploration felt safer and more approachable, making them strong entry points for broader participation.

The Solution: A Page That Encourages Looking Closer

We redesigned the Subject page to feel less like a dead end and more like a crossroads.

  • Metadata was surfaced to provide immediate scientific context
  • Related subjects created natural exploration pathways
  • Tagging was positioned as a low-friction way to contribute insight
  • Key tools were brought closer to the subject itself

Rather than rewarding speed or volume, the page rewards attention.

Testing: Learning Where Expectations Break

Over two months, we ran selective testing with two projects, coordinating closely with team leads. Every participant had access to a feedback form, allowing us to gather both qualitative reactions and detailed usability notes.

What We Learned

  • The new tagging system was widely preferred and clearly lowered participation barriers
  • However, interaction patterns didn't always match user expectations shaped by other platforms
  • This mismatch created friction and highlighted areas that need refactoring, which I am guiding with the development team

We also learned that the commenting experience was problematic: it wasn't clearly signposted, and implementing it as a modal blocked the subject itself.

These issues stemmed from leadership decisions which made the page less focused on exploration and more focused on discussion—a tension that will need to be revisited.

Launch: What We'll Be Watching For

This project will launch soon to the wider Zooniverse Talk pages.

Post-launch, we'll be closely monitoring:

  • Where users hesitate or abandon exploration paths
  • Whether tagging feels intuitive without instruction
  • How often volunteers move between Subject and Classification pages
  • Whether comments enhance understanding—or distract from the subject
  • Feedback from advanced users on metadata usefulness and placement

This launch is as much about learning as it is about shipping.

The Outcome (Anticipated)

By giving volunteers more context and clearer pathways, the Subject page can evolve from a single interaction point into a place of insight and collaboration.

Casual users gain confidence to explore.

Power users gain efficiency.

Researchers gain better-informed contributions.

And crucially, scientific work remains careful, contextual, and collaborative.

Lessons Learned

  • Product complexity must be shared, not assumed: Leadership decisions sometimes underestimated how volunteers actually use the platform
  • Clear communication is a design responsibility: I need to better articulate user workflows and risks earlier to avoid fundamental UX disagreements
  • Feedback increases with exposure: I expect significantly more insight once the full pages are live

This project reinforced that designing for discovery means designing for restraint. Sometimes the most responsible UX decision is not adding more incentives—but giving curiosity the space it needs to grow.
