Volumetric Data Viewer

Role: Product Designer
Scope: 3D data visualization and interaction
Timeline: 6 weeks
Outcome: Enabled work on complex 3D datasets, laid groundwork for future medical projects

Distilling Complexity for Beginners

To understand how the brain produces thought, emotion, and movement, scientists map dense networks formed by billions of neurons. These connections live inside massive 3D datasets that traditional 2D tools cannot show.

Researchers expect that level of complexity. Participants do not.

This project asked a simple question: how can non-experts contribute to advanced brain research without lowering scientific standards?

The Tension

Volumetric brain data is technical. It requires spatial reasoning, precision, and patience.

Zooniverse focuses on access. It invites everyday people into serious science.

We could not simplify the task itself. We could only simplify how people learned it.

The Challenge

How can we make deeply technical 3D data approachable without losing accuracy?

Volunteers needed to trace individual neuronal pathways carefully. Researchers needed usable data. The interface had to support both.

Expected Hurdles

Complex Data

3D scans contain layers of information that overwhelm new users.

Steep Learning Curve

The task is difficult. People need clear ways to undo mistakes.

Lack of Context

Without orientation cues, users without neuroscience backgrounds struggle to understand what they are seeing or where the data fits.

Goals

We set out to design a viewer that could:

  • Help volunteers trace neuronal pathways accurately
  • Break massive datasets into smaller cubes
  • Feel simple and familiar, even when the task was complex
  • Support future volumetric projects beyond neuroscience

The interface needed to teach through interaction, not long instructions.

Guiding Principles

Intuitive

Lower friction for first-time participants

Familiar

Use patterns from existing 3D tools

Future-Proof

Support different datasets over time

Forgiving

Let participants undo their mistakes

Research & Design: Translating 3D Space

Our lead engineer identified open-source components as a starting point. I layered in Zooniverse's design language and interaction patterns.

Key decisions included:

  • Randomized colors to separate traced paths
  • Collapsible slice views with matching color-coded axes
  • Clear orientation markers to anchor users in 3D space

I drew from common 3D design software and reused interactions people might already know. Responsive layouts kept the tool usable across devices.

The goal was not to make users experts. It was to help them feel capable.

Testing: Letting Confusion Surface

We began with internal alpha testing to confirm performance across browsers.

Once stable, we moved to beta testing that mirrored real volunteer workflows. We gave minimal guidance so we could watch natural behavior.

We learned:

  • Users struggled to annotate multiple marks efficiently
  • The "Add a New Mark" action needed a visible button, not a hidden shortcut
  • Accessibility needed another review based on feedback

Fixing these issues improved discoverability and reduced hesitation. Small changes increased confidence.

Launch & Impact

The first dataset completed with 35,000 classifications in three months.

Each task took three to ten minutes. Some sessions lasted nearly an hour. Despite the difficulty, users stayed engaged and annotated every segment they could find.

Their work reduced researcher effort and sped up neural pathway mapping. A single researcher might spend years on a task like this.

The Outcome

The Volumetric Viewer marked a shift for the Zooniverse platform.

For the first time, volunteers worked directly with true 3D datasets. This opened the door to projects in neuroscience, pathology, and medical imaging.

A specialized research task became collaborative and open to the public.

The viewer also created a foundation for future CT and MRI-based studies. It expanded what citizen science could support.

Design connected complex data with everyday curiosity.

Lessons Learned

This project depended on close collaboration between engineering and design. Scientific requirements, performance limits, and usability had to align.

Working with researchers, we learned to decide which complexity must stay visible for accuracy and where the interface could carry the burden. Early testing helped us avoid building features that did not improve understanding.

We also saw the value of designing for scale. Flexible interaction patterns and a strong technical base allowed the viewer to extend beyond one neuroscience project into future CT and MRI work.

This project strengthened our approach to building scientific tools that are rigorous and usable. It set a clear direction for expanding into more technical domains without losing accessibility.
