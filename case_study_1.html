Establishing the Zooniverse Design System

Role: Product Designer
Scope: Design system, systems thinking
Timeline: 8 weeks
Outcome: Unified design language, reduced design debt

From Design Debt to Intentional Design

Zooniverse is the world's largest platform for people-powered research, enabling millions of volunteers to contribute to real science across disciplines like astronomy and ecology. Over more than a decade, the platform grew organically. Pages and features were built incrementally by different teams, with UI decisions driven largely in code rather than by a shared design foundation—resulting in significant design debt across the platform.

As the engineering team prepared for a front-end migration to a newer architecture, we used this moment as a strategic inflection point. Rather than simply re-platforming existing UI, I chose to use the homepage as the starting point for a new framework moving forward.

This case study documents a multi-year effort to establish a scalable design system for the entire product.

The Problem: Design Debt Meets Legacy Architecture

Zooniverse's UI had been shaped by years of well-intentioned, piecemeal implementation:

  • Developers built pages independently, often without design input
  • Similar components existed in multiple variations
  • Typography, spacing, and color usage varied from page to page
  • New features required re-solving the same design problems

This fragmentation was reinforced by the previous front-end architecture, which made reuse difficult and consistency hard to maintain.

Reframing the Goal

Instead of asking, "What should the landing page look like?" we reframed the effort around higher-leverage questions:

  • What foundational elements are missing from the platform?
  • How can a single decision scale across dozens of pages?
  • How do we align design decisions with the new front-end architecture?

Establishing the Foundations

The migration created a rare opportunity: define standards once and bake them directly into the new architecture.

Visual Language

We began with a comprehensive audit of existing pages to understand where patterns aligned—and where they conflicted. From there, we defined:

  • A clear typographic hierarchy that made dense research content more accessible
  • Rules that worked with user-generated content of varying size and quality
  • Spacing and layout standards that unified the interface and improved scanning for key information

These decisions replaced guesswork for developers with defaults that could be enforced through components, not documentation alone.

Accessibility as a Baseline

Accessibility issues were a major source of hidden design debt. Rather than fixing contrast or focus states ad hoc, accessibility was considered from the start of the new system, ensuring every component inherited these standards by default.

Components as a Bridge Between Design and Architecture

With foundations in place, we focused on high-impact components that mapped cleanly onto the new front-end structure:

  • Buttons and calls to action that previously varied in size, color, and behavior
  • Card patterns used for projects, subjects, and collections
  • Classifier-essential tools such as zoom, rotate, invert, and draw

Each component was designed to be:

  • Reusable and modular within the new architecture and future pages
  • Flexible enough to support diverse scientific research
  • Universal enough that users would recognize functionality even as visual styles evolved

As components were adopted, consistency stopped being a design goal and became a byproduct of implementation.

Documentation & Adoption

A system only works if teams use it. Documentation focused on intent as much as implementation:

  • Clear usage guidance to prevent misuse
  • A shared vocabulary between design and engineering

By aligning the design system with the new front-end platform, adoption became the path of least resistance.

Applying the System: The Zooniverse Landing Page

The redesigned landing page became the first major surface fully built on the new architecture and design system.

Previously, the homepage struggled to clearly explain what Zooniverse was, why it mattered, and how people could participate. Using system components, we focused on:

  • Immediate, mission-led clarity
  • Human-centered storytelling through real researchers and projects
  • Clear pathways to participation

Impact

  • Reduced design and technical debt across the platform
  • Clear guidance for future implementation
  • Faster iteration through reusable, system-aligned components
  • Improved accessibility, usability, and SEO
  • A homepage that felt familiar through shared patterns, while modernized to reflect contemporary design standards

The new homepage helped visitors understand the mission quickly and feel connected to the science, while the design system ensured future pages could be built with consistency by default.

Reflection

For a platform as large and long-lived as Zooniverse, consistency isn't about polish—it's about sustainability. The move to a new architecture made it possible to reset years of accumulated debt, and the design system ensured that reset could scale with intention.

This project taught me that the most impactful design work often happens at the systems level—not in individual screens, but in the decisions that make every future screen easier to build right.

By investing in foundations rather than just features, we created infrastructure that will serve the platform for years to come.

Lessons Learned

  • Cross-functional alignment early matters: This project touched infrastructure, content, and marketing. Close collaboration ensured design decisions were technically feasible, on-brand, and rooted in real user needs.
  • Clarity before inspiration: While storytelling motivates, users must first understand what Zooniverse is before they can feel inspired by it.
  • Systems thinking scales better than pixel-pushing: The highest-leverage design work often happens at the component and pattern level, not in individual layouts.
  • Documentation is design work: A system only succeeds when teams understand not just how to use it, but why it exists.

This project reinforced that designing for sustainability means building systems that make the right thing the easy thing.
